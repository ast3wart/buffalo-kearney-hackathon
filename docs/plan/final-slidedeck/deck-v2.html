Problem

Owners do not know the health of the equipment their plants run on

A typical situation

The plant follows its maintenance schedule: bearings greased, belts changed, oil sampled.

Nobody is watching the 20-year-old drive in the cabinet. The manufacturer stopped making it years ago, and there is no spare.

It fails. The line stops.

Appendix A1 · how the numbers are calculated

Every formula, and which inputs are assumptions

NumberFormulaInputs that are judgment calls
Part healthLife used = age ÷ typical life. Replace ASAP if the life left is shorter than the time to get one; replace soon at 75% of life used; else working.Typical life per part family
Health score proposedWorking 100, replace soon 50, replace ASAP 0. Weighted average over identified parts; line stoppers count double.The point values and the double weight. Not in the app yet.
Carrying cost avoidedExcess spares (10–25% of stock value) × carrying cost (12–30% a year)Excess share
Rush premiums avoidedYearly rush premiums × 10–30% avoidedShare avoided
Sister-spare purchases avoidedFor each part a sister plant can cover: chance of failure per year × (part value − transfer cost)Failure odds by age: 3% / 6% / 8% a year for under 10 / 10–20 / over 20 years
Planned vs emergencyFor each at-risk part: chance of failure × replacement cost × emergency premium (10–60%), then halvedPremium and the 50% haircut
Whole-plant quoteThe two part-level savings ÷ share of the plant scanned (machines scanned ÷ estimated machines, at 0.55 per employee)Machines per employee
Downtime avoided (not in savings)Hours saved by the spare × margin per hour × 40–80% not made up later × chance of one failure in 3 years, discounted at 10%. Never scaled up.Outage cap (10–25 operating days), share not made up, 3-year horizon
