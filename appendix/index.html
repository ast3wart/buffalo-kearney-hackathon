Install records: the ~28% formula

How many parts come with a known install date? No survey measures it directly, so we combined the Velocity estimates: From Velocity · Manufacturing Maintenance Data Adoption

P(install record) = P(CMMS) × [ P(part level) × completeness  +  P(machine level) × P(part is original) × completeness ]
                  = 0.60     × [ 0.15 × 0.7                +  0.85 × 0.6 × 0.7 ]

05

Health score formula

Health = 100 × the chance the part survives the next 12 months, from a Weibull proportional-hazards survival model (the reliability-engineering standard).

effective age  a = life_used × rated_life
life_used      = self-reported (fan/capacitor life from the drive)            if available
               = age × heat_factor / rated_life                               otherwise
heat_factor    = 2 ^ ((T − T_ref) / 10)      (life halves per +10 °C; half of today's excess counted for the past)
hazard mult. m = exp( ln(20) × anomaly_severity ) × (1 + 0.3 × faults_12m)     (faults capped at 5)
η = 1.1 × rated_life,  β = 3

health        = 100 × exp( −m × ((a + 1)^β − a^β) / η^β )          floored at 1
remaining life = (a^β + η^β × ln 2 / m)^(1/β) − a                    (conditional median)
range          = ± 15% / 30% / 50%  for high / medium / low confidence
StatusRule
Replace ASAPhealth ≤ 10, or getting a replacement takes longer than the life left
Replace soon≥ 75% of life used, but there is still time to get one
Workingotherwise

Rated life comes from the manufacturer manual / knowledge base. T_ref: drives 55 °C, PLCs/HMIs 45 °C, power supplies 50 °C. Order-by dates use the health model's conservative remaining life (below).

06

Anomaly detection statistics

Each monitored part learns its own normal over 30 days, then every daily summary is checked by standard statistical process control:

expected temp:  (T − ambient) = a + c × load        (least squares on the 30-day baseline)
residual r      = actual − expected
EWMA            z_t = 0.2 r_t + 0.8 z_{t−1};  alarm beyond 3σ limits        → sudden shifts (failing fan)
CUSUM           S_t = max(0, S_{t−1} + r_t/σ − 0.5);  alarm at S > 5        → slow drift (aging capacitors)
trend           slope of r over time, t-test;  alarm at > 1 °C/month, t > 3 → warming
peers           robust z vs same-model parts (median / MAD);  alarm at |z| > 3
motor current   Mahalanobis distance on RMS/load + sideband energy;  alarm at > 3
severity        1 − exp(−(stat − alarm)/alarm) per detector;  anomaly_severity = max (0–1)
judged on the last 7 days

Toledo line-stopper drive with a failing fan (planted, simulated sensor data): EWMA crosses its limit the week the step starts. Measured false-alarm rate: ~1 in 40 monitored parts.

07

Downtime avoided and output protected

Downtime is the number plants feel. We count it two ways, and only for spare rescues we actually found (a line-stopping part whose spare sits at a sister plant). Never scaled up to the whole plant, never in the fee.

hours down without spare = min(lead time to buy one, outage cap 10–25 operating days) × operating hours/day
hours down with spare    = transfer (1–2 days) + swap (2–8 h)
at stake (one failure)   = (hours without − hours with) × line $/hour × share of output never made up (40–80%)

OUTPUT PROTECTED   = Σ at stake                                  (Toledo: $622k, 668 h of stoppage)
DOWNTIME AVOIDED   = Σ P(fails within 3 yrs) × at stake, discounted at 10%, per year   (Toledo: ~$77k/yr, $12–205k)
EXPOSURE / YEAR    = P(fail this year) × hours down × $/hour × unrecovered share        (line-stopper table)

P(fail this year)  = 1 − health/100     (the part's own health score: age, heat, sensor anomalies, faults)
                   = age table below     (only for parts with no health evidence)

Fallback failure odds by age, for parts with no health score: 0–10 yrs 2–5%/yr, 10–20 yrs 4–8%, 20+ yrs 6–10% From Velocity · money thread #4. Transfer time and cost From Velocity · #20 sister-plant transfer. Line $/hour from plant revenue and operating hours From Velocity · #1 plant baseline.

CapEx cliff and order-by dates

CapEx in year Y   = Σ replacement cost of control parts expected to reach end of life in Y  (+ migration engineering if discontinued)
replacement cost  = market price from trusted listings, else type fallback;  migration = engineering hours × integrator rate
failure date      = today + health model's conservative remaining life   (else install year + typical life)
order-by          = failure date − lead time − 14-day buffer

13

The $1.4T on the problem slide

What it is: unplanned downtime costs the world's 500 largest companies about $1.4 trillion a year, 11% of their revenue (Siemens, The True Cost of Downtime, 2024). From Velocity · Manufacturing Equipment Risk Costs

How to say it: it is a cost to the largest companies, not the size of a market, and not specific to mid-market plants. For smaller firms, the same Velocity research found 52% of smaller US manufacturers put an hour of downtime at $50k or more, and 44% have an equipment interruption every month.

14

How much automation is end-of-life (83%)

What it is: one real plant audit published by Siemens (2025): 699 of 843 automation assets, from 17 manufacturers, were discontinued or end-of-life. From Velocity · Manufacturing Equipment Risk Costs

Why we don't headline it: it is one plant, from a vendor that sells upgrades, not a national rate. Siemens' other published audits found 35% (3,686 filling/packaging assets), 59% (914 process assets) and 60% (388 utility assets). From Velocity · Manufacturing Equipment Risk Costs

What we claim: a large share of the automation in older plants is past its supported life, often a third to most of it. Our own demo portfolio, built on real part numbers and lifecycle data, has 32% of critical parts discontinued or end-of-life. No survey measures a national rate, so we never quote one.
